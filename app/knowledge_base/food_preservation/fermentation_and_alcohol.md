# Fermentation, Food Preservation and Alcohol Production
# Source: Historical practical methods + Food science
# Confidence: HIGH — Used safely for thousands of years

## Why Fermentation Matters for Survival

Fermentation is one of the most critical skills for long-term survival.
It solves three problems simultaneously:
1. Preserves food that would otherwise rot
2. Makes food safer (acids kill pathogens)
3. Produces alcohol — medicine, disinfectant, morale, trade currency

Every civilisation independently discovered fermentation.
You have wild yeast and bacteria on your skin and in the air right now.
You do not need to buy anything to start fermenting.

---

## The Science (Simple Version)

Yeast eats sugar → produces alcohol + CO2
Bacteria eat sugar → produces lactic acid (lacto-fermentation)
Bacteria eat alcohol → produces acetic acid (vinegar)

Salt and acidity create environments where good microbes thrive
and dangerous ones cannot survive.
This is why properly fermented food is SAFER than fresh food.

## SECTION 1: LACTO-FERMENTATION (Vegetables)

No equipment needed. No heat needed. Works everywhere.
The most important fermentation to learn first.

### Basic Sauerkraut (Fermented Cabbage)

Why sauerkraut first:
- Lasts months without refrigeration
- Vitamin C prevents scurvy (critical in winter)
- Probiotics improve digestion and immunity
- Requires: cabbage + salt. Nothing else.

Ingredients:
- 1 medium cabbage (about 1kg)
- 20g non-iodised salt (2% of cabbage weight)
- Do NOT use iodised salt — kills the bacteria you need

Process:
1. Remove outer leaves, set aside
2. Shred cabbage finely — 3-5mm strips
3. Place in large bowl, add salt
4. Massage and squeeze VIGOROUSLY for 10 minutes
   Cabbage will release water — this is the brine
5. Pack tightly into clean jar or crock
   Push down hard — no air pockets
   Brine should rise above cabbage
6. Weigh cabbage down below brine:
   - Use a smaller jar filled with water as weight
   - Or use the reserved outer leaves as a cap
7. Cover with cloth to keep bugs out (not airtight)
8. Leave at room temperature 15-25°C (ideal 18-22°C)
9. Taste from day 3 onwards
10. Ready when pleasantly sour — usually 1-4 weeks
11. Once at desired sourness: cover tightly, store in cool place
    Lasts 6+ months properly stored

What you will see:
- Day 1-2: Bubbles forming (CO2 — good sign)
- White foam on surface — skim off, this is normal
- Possible white film (kahm yeast) — harmless, skim off
- Smell: pleasantly sour, like vinegar — never rotten

DISCARD if: slimy texture, rotten smell, black/pink/fuzzy mould

### Fermented Any Vegetable

Same principle works for: carrots, turnips, radishes, beets,
green beans, peppers, cucumbers (= real pickles)

The 2% salt rule:
- Weigh your vegetable
- Use 2% of that weight in non-iodised salt
- Pack, submerge, ferment same as sauerkraut

Mixed vegetable ferments are excellent — use what you have.

## SECTION 2: VINEGAR PRODUCTION

Essential product. Uses:
- Food preservation (pickling)
- Cleaning and disinfection
- Medicine (wound cleaning diluted, ear infections)
- Removing mineral deposits
- Trade item

### Making Vinegar from Alcohol

Any alcohol below 15% ABV can be converted to vinegar.
The bacteria that make vinegar (acetobacter) need oxygen.

Method:
1. Dilute any alcohol to 5-10% ABV if stronger
   (wine is perfect as-is, spirits need diluting)
2. Pour into wide-mouthed container (maximum surface area)
3. Cover with cloth — allows air in, keeps bugs out
4. Add vinegar mother if available:
   - Unpasteurised apple cider vinegar contains live culture
   - Slimy disc-like culture that may form naturally
   - Not strictly necessary — wild acetobacter is in the air
5. Stir once daily for air exposure
6. Temperature: 20-30°C ideal
7. Ready in 4-8 weeks — taste until sharp enough
8. Filter through cloth, bottle, seal
9. Pasteurise for storage: heat to 60°C for 30 min (kills bacteria but keeps flavour)

Without alcohol: make vinegar from fruit
1. Crush fruit, add water, add small amount of sugar if available
2. Ferment to alcohol first (2-4 weeks)
3. Then continue to vinegar stage (4-8 more weeks)

## SECTION 3: ALCOHOL PRODUCTION

This section covers safe production of alcohol for:
- Medicine and wound disinfection (highest priority use)
- Preservation (alcohol preserves food and medicine)
- Fuel (alcohol burns — lamps, heating, engine additive)
- Morale and social cohesion (legitimate survival consideration)
- Trade currency (extremely high value in collapse scenario)

### Safety First — Read This Before Proceeding

METHANOL WARNING — This is the only serious danger in fermentation:
Methanol (wood alcohol) is toxic. Small amounts cause blindness.
Larger amounts cause death.

CRITICAL FACTS:
1. Fermentation of sugars by yeast produces ETHANOL — safe to drink
2. Methanol is produced in tiny amounts in ALL fermentation
3. These tiny amounts are NOT dangerous — your body processes them
4. The only way to get dangerous methanol concentrations:
   - Distilling and collecting the FIRST portion (foreshots) in large quantity
   - Using methanol-containing starting materials (wood pulp, certain pectin-rich fruits in distillation)
   - Industrial processes — NOT home fermentation
5. BEER and WINE as produced by fermentation are safe
6. Basic distillation with proper foreshots discarding is safe
7. The "going blind from moonshine" stories are almost always:
   - From added methanol (fraud/crime) not fermentation
   - From improperly made or contaminated spirits
   - Historical industrial alcohol sold as drinking alcohol

SAFE PRACTICE:
- Fermented drinks (beer, wine, cider) require NO special knowledge — just ferment
- Distillation: always discard first 50ml per 20 litres of wash
- Never use radiator fluid, wood alcohol, or industrial solvents
- Use glass, food-grade plastic, or stainless for all vessels
- Never seal fermenting vessel airtight — CO2 pressure can explode containers

### Understanding Alcohol Strength

Fermentation naturally stops at 12-15% ABV
(yeast dies in its own waste at this concentration)

Beer: typically 4-8% ABV
Wine: typically 10-15% ABV
Mead: typically 10-15% ABV
Distilled spirits: 40-95% ABV (requires distillation)

For medicinal disinfection: 60%+ ABV needed (distilled)
For consumption: fermented drinks (no distillation needed)
For wound cleaning: dilute spirits to 70% with clean water (optimal)

## MAKING BEER

### Simple Grain Beer — No Equipment Needed

Ingredients:
- Any grain: barley (best), wheat, corn, oats, rye
- Water
- Yeast source (see below)
- Optional: hops or other bittering herbs (yarrow, mugwort, juniper)

Step 1 — Malting (converts starch to sugar):
1. Soak grain in water 24-48 hours until swollen
2. Spread in thin layer, keep moist, temperature 15-20°C
3. Grain will sprout — wait until shoots are 2/3 grain length
4. This takes 3-5 days
5. DRY the sprouted grain (malted barley = malt):
   - Sun dry, or dry near fire at low heat under 50°C
   - Kiln at higher temp for darker beer (changes flavour)
6. Grind malt coarsely (stone grinder, two rocks)

Step 2 — Mashing (extract sugars):
1. Mix ground malt with hot water (65-70°C) — ratio 1:3 malt to water
2. Maintain temperature 65°C for 1 hour (wrap container in insulation)
   This activates enzymes that convert remaining starch to sugar
3. You now have "sweet wort" — drain liquid off grain
4. Rinse grain with more hot water to extract remaining sugars

Step 3 — Boiling:
1. Boil wort 60-90 minutes
2. Boiling: sterilises, concentrates sugars, drives off off-flavours
3. Add bittering herbs last 30 minutes if desired:
   - Hops (if available) — classic beer bittering
   - Yarrow — traditional pre-hop bittering, pleasant
   - Juniper berries — Nordic tradition, works well
4. Cool to below 30°C before adding yeast

Step 4 — Fermentation:
1. Pour cooled wort into clean vessel
2. Add yeast (see yeast sourcing below)
3. Cover with cloth — allows CO2 out, keeps bugs in
4. Ferment at room temperature 1-2 weeks
5. Bubbling slows — beer is ready when flat for 2-3 days
6. Bottle or drink fresh
7. Naturally carbonated if bottled with small residual sugar

### Yeast Sourcing (Wild Capture)

You do not need to buy yeast. Wild yeast is everywhere.

Method 1 — Wild capture:
1. Mix flour + water to thick paste
2. Leave uncovered at room temperature
3. Stir twice daily
4. In 3-7 days it will bubble and smell yeasty (sourdough starter)
5. This is wild yeast — use it to start fermentation

Method 2 — Fruit yeast:
1. Crush unwashed ripe fruit (grapes, plums, berries)
2. Wild yeast lives on fruit skin
3. Simply leave crushed fruit — fermentation begins in 1-3 days

Method 3 — Save yeast from previous batch:
1. Yeast settles to bottom of fermentation vessel (trub/lees)
2. Keep a few tablespoons of this sediment
3. Refrigerate or keep cool
4. Use to start next batch immediately
5. This is how breweries maintained yeast for centuries

## MAKING WINE AND CIDER

### Fruit Wine — Simplest Alcohol

Any fruit can make wine. Sugar content determines final strength.

Ingredients:
- 1kg ripe fruit (crush and use juice)
- 1L water
- Optional: 200g sugar (increases strength and preserves)
- Wild yeast from fruit skin, or captured wild yeast

Process:
1. Crush fruit — do not wash (preserves wild yeast)
2. Add water and sugar if using
3. Pour into vessel — fill only 2/3 full (allows bubbling)
4. Cover with cloth
5. Fermentation begins in 1-3 days (bubbling, fruity smell)
6. Stir daily for first week to reintroduce yeast and oxygen
7. After 1 week: strain out solids, continue fermenting liquid
8. Ready in 2-6 weeks when bubbling stops
9. Siphon off clear liquid, leaving sediment behind
10. Bottle — drink young or age for better flavour

Best fruits for wine:
- Grapes (classic, highest sugar)
- Apples and pears (= cider, easy and productive)
- Elderberries (very good — also medicinal)
- Blackberries, raspberries, strawberries
- Plums, damsons, sloes
- In tropics: banana, mango, pineapple, coconut (palm wine)

### Cider (Apple/Pear Wine)

Simplest of all fruit fermentations:
1. Crush or press apples — collect juice
2. Leave in covered vessel
3. Wild yeast on apple skin starts fermentation in 2-3 days
4. Ferment 2-4 weeks
5. Ready when bubbling stops and liquid is clear
6. Siphon, bottle, drink

Dry cider is naturally preserved — 5-8% ABV
Can last months in cool dark storage

## MAKING MEAD (HONEY WINE)

Mead is historically the most universal alcoholic drink.
Made wherever bees exist. Simple. Excellent.

Ingredients:
- 1 part honey
- 3-4 parts water (adjust for desired strength)
- Yeast

Process:
1. Warm water slightly (not hot — kills yeast and nutrients)
2. Dissolve honey completely in water (hydromel/must)
3. Cool to room temperature
4. Add yeast (wild yeast, bread yeast, fruit yeast)
5. Pour into vessel, fill 2/3 full
6. Cover with cloth
7. Ferment 2-6 weeks
8. Stronger honey ratio = stronger mead = longer fermentation
9. Mead is ready when bubbling stops and liquid begins to clear
10. Siphon off clear liquid, leaving sediment
11. Age improves mead dramatically — 3+ months ideal
12. Drink fresh or bottle for aging

Mead variations:
- Metheglin: add herbs and spices (medicinal mead)
- Melomel: add fruit
- Cyser: add apple juice
- Bochet: caramelise honey before fermentation (nutty flavour)

Medicinal meads:
- Elder flower mead: anti-viral, respiratory health
- Ginger mead: anti-nausea, digestive aid
- Garlic mead (yes, really): potent antibiotic properties
- Rose hip mead: vitamin C, immune support

## BASIC DISTILLATION

IMPORTANT: Distillation laws vary. In collapse scenario legality is irrelevant.
This section covers safe production of high-strength spirits for
medicinal use, disinfection, fuel, and preservation.

### What Distillation Does

Alcohol boils at 78.4°C. Water boils at 100°C.
Heat the fermented liquid — alcohol vapour rises first.
Cool that vapour — it condenses back to liquid.
This concentrated liquid is spirits/distilled alcohol.

### Simple Still Construction

Pot still (simplest):
1. Copper or stainless pot with tight-fitting lid
2. Pipe or tube fitted to lid (outlet for vapour)
3. Pipe coiled in cold water bath (condenser coil)
4. Collection vessel at end of coil
5. Heat source under pot (fire, coals)

Materials:
- Copper is best (removes sulfur compounds — important for taste and safety)
- Stainless steel is acceptable
- DO NOT use lead, zinc, galvanised metal, or plastics — toxic contamination
- Seal all joints with food-grade material (flour paste works temporarily)

### Running a Safe Distillation

1. Fill pot no more than 2/3 full with fermented wash
2. Heat slowly and steadily — do not boil hard
3. Keep cold water flowing over condenser
4. Temperature rises — vapour begins at 78-85°C range

Foreshots (first portion — discard):
- First 50ml per 20 litres of wash coming over = foreshots
- Contains methanol and other heads compounds
- Discard this — do not drink, do not use for medical
- Can use for cleaning, lamp fuel
- After discarding foreshots: product is safe

Hearts (main collection — keep):
- Sweet, clean smelling distillate
- This is your drinkable/medicinal product
- Collect until smell changes to harsh/unpleasant

Tails (end portion — optional separate):
- Becomes harsh, oily, less pleasant
- Collect separately — can redistill or use for fuel
- Not dangerous but lower quality

Testing strength without equipment:
- Shake bottle vigorously — observe bubbles
- Large bubbles that disappear quickly = high alcohol
- Small bubbles that persist = lower alcohol
- Burn test: pour small amount, ignite — burns blue = high strength
  Does not ignite = too weak

### Medicinal Alcohol Use

70% ABV is optimal for wound disinfection (kills bacteria and viruses best)
90%+ is actually LESS effective (denatures too fast, doesn't penetrate)

Diluting spirits to 70%:
- If you have 90% spirits: mix 70ml spirits + 30ml clean water
- If you have 40% spirits: this is slightly below optimal but works for surface disinfection

Uses:
- Wound cleaning: pour over wound, do not rub
- Instrument sterilisation: soak 10+ minutes
- Tincture making: extract medicinal plant compounds
- Preservation: herbs, medicines in alcohol last years

## TROUBLESHOOTING

### Fermentation not starting:
- Temperature too cold — move to warmer location (18-25°C ideal)
- Not enough wild yeast — add crushed unwashed fruit skin
- Salt too high — inhibits yeast (check ratio)
- Wait another 2 days — wild fermentation is slower than commercial yeast

### Off smells:
- Rotten egg (sulfur): stir to add oxygen, often resolves itself
- Vinegar smell too early: acetobacter contamination — seal more tightly
- Truly rotten, putrid: discard and start again with cleaner equipment

### White film on surface (kahm yeast):
- Harmless, just aesthetically unpleasant
- Skim off, ensure liquid is still submerged, continue
- Not mould — mould is fuzzy, raised, coloured

### Mould (fuzzy, raised, pink/black/green):
- Discard if mould has penetrated — do not eat
- If mould only on surface and product below is fine: depends on product
- Wine/beer: skim, taste, usually fine if sour not rotten
- Vegetable ferments: discard if soft, slimy beneath mould

### Distillate smells bad:
- Foreshots not properly discarded — discard first 50ml and continue
- Still made of wrong materials — copper contamination unlikely but zinc/lead possible
- Fermentation produced excessive sulfur — copper still fixes this
- Run again (redistill) for cleaner product
