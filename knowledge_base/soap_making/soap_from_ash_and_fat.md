# Soap Making from Ash and Fat
Source: Survivor Library + Historical Practical Methods
Confidence: HIGH — Used by millions for thousands of years

## Why Soap is Civilization-Critical
Soap saves more lives than almost any other technology.
Cholera, typhoid, dysentery — all preventable with handwashing.
Making soap is one of the highest-value early skills in any collapse scenario.
Materials needed are available almost everywhere: wood ash + any fat.

---

## Understanding the Chemistry

Fat (triglycerides) + Strong Alkali (lye) → Soap + Glycerin

This reaction is called saponification.
The alkali breaks apart fat molecules and reforms them into soap molecules.
The resulting soap has one end that attracts water and one end that attracts grease.
This is what makes soap clean — it pulls oil and dirt off surfaces.

Two types of lye used historically:
- Potassium hydroxide (KOH) from wood ash leaching → soft soap (paste)
- Sodium hydroxide (NaOH) from certain mineral sources → hard soap (bar)

Wood ash gives potassium lye → produces soft or liquid soap
This is what you can make anywhere there is wood.

## Step 1 — Making Lye from Wood Ash

### Which Ash to Use
Best: Hardwood ash (oak, hickory, maple, ash tree, beech)
Good: Most wood ash from clean wood fires
Avoid: Ash from coal, painted wood, treated lumber, or plastics

### Equipment Needed
- Container with holes in bottom (wooden barrel, clay pot with hole, woven basket lined with straw)
- Collection container below
- Clean rainwater (soft water — not mineral-rich hard water)
- Cheesecloth or straw for filtering

### Process
1. Fill leaching container loosely with dry wood ash
2. Tamp down gently — not packed tight
3. Place straw or cloth in bottom to filter out ash particles
4. Position collection container below the drain hole
5. Pour clean rainwater slowly over ash
   Use cold or lukewarm water only — hot water produces weaker lye
6. Let water percolate through ash slowly
7. Collect the dark brown liquid that drips through — this is raw lye solution
8. Run collected lye back through ash 2-3 more times to concentrate it

### Testing Lye Strength — Critical Step
Lye must be correct strength for soap to form.
Too weak: soap won't set up
Too strong: soap will be caustic and burn skin

Traditional egg test:
- Float a fresh egg in the lye solution
- Correct strength: egg floats with surface area of a 50-cent coin showing above surface
- Too weak: egg sinks or barely floats (run through ash again)
- Too strong: egg floats too high (dilute with clean water)

Feather test:
- Dip a chicken feather in lye solution
- Correct strength: feather begins to dissolve within 30 seconds
- Too weak: feather unchanged

If lye is too weak: concentrate by boiling slowly to evaporate water
If lye is too strong: dilute with clean water and retest

## Step 2 — Preparing Fat

### Types of Fat
Best: Rendered animal fat — lard (pork), tallow (beef/sheep), bear fat
Good: Any animal fat including chicken fat, goose fat
Possible: Coconut oil, olive oil, vegetable oils (produce softer soap)
Avoid: Fat from carnivores (poor soap quality)

### Rendering Fat (Purifying Raw Animal Fat)

Raw fat contains blood, water, and tissue that must be removed:

1. Cut raw fat into small pieces — remove meat, gristle, blood
2. Add equal volume of water to fat in pot
3. Heat slowly to simmer — do not boil hard
4. Stir occasionally for 1-2 hours
5. Fat melts and separates from impurities
6. Pour through cloth strainer into clean container
7. Allow to cool completely — fat solidifies and floats on top
8. Remove solidified fat cake from water
9. Discard water layer (contains impurities)
10. Rendered fat should be white or pale yellow, clean-smelling

Repeat rendering 2-3 times for cleanest fat.
Cleaner fat produces better, longer-lasting soap.

## Step 3 — Making the Soap

### Proportions (Traditional Method)
The ratio matters — this is tested over centuries:
- 3 parts fat (by volume)
- 1 part lye solution (of correct tested strength)

Adjust if soap doesn't set — more lye if too soft, more fat if too caustic.

### Safety
Lye is strongly caustic. It will burn skin and eyes.
Work outdoors or in well-ventilated area.
Protect hands with cloth or gloves if available.
If lye contacts skin: flush immediately with large amounts of water.
Do not use metal containers — lye reacts with some metals.
Use clay, wood, or iron (cast iron is acceptable).

### Cooking Method (Cold Process not reliable without precise measurements)

1. Heat rendered fat in clay or iron pot to liquid but not smoking
2. Remove from direct heat
3. Carefully add lye solution to fat — NEVER add fat to lye
4. Stir slowly and constantly in one direction
5. Mixture will become cloudy and begin to thicken — this is trace
6. Keep stirring — this can take 20 minutes to 2 hours
7. Trace: mixture is ready when drizzling it on surface leaves brief trail
8. At trace: add any desired additions (herbs, ash for scrubbing, clay)
9. Pour into molds — wooden boxes lined with damp cloth work well
10. Cover with cloth or board to retain heat
11. Allow to sit 24-48 hours undisturbed

### Curing
Soap must cure before use — this is not optional.
During cure: saponification completes, pH drops from caustic to safe.
Minimum cure time: 4 weeks
Better: 6-8 weeks
Test readiness: touch with wet tongue — no sting means ready

During curing:
- Store in cool dry place with air circulation
- Do not stack — allow air on all sides
- White powder on surface (soda ash) is harmless — wipe off before use
- Cut into bars after first week if still soft

## Testing Finished Soap

pH test with indicator plant:
- Boil red cabbage in water — liquid turns purple
- Add drops of soap solution — should turn blue-green (mildly alkaline, safe)
- If turns yellow: too alkaline, cure longer
- If stays purple: neutral, ready to use

Lather test:
- Wet hands, rub soap between them
- Should produce stable lather easily in 10 seconds
- If no lather: soap failed — insufficient lye, re-cook with more lye

Skin feel:
- Wash hands with soap, rinse well
- Should feel clean without stinging or irritation
- Some slip feeling is normal
- Stinging or burning means more curing needed

## Troubleshooting

Soap too soft after 48 hours:
- Too little lye or lye too weak
- Cook mixture again, adding more lye solution gradually

Soap separated into layers:
- Lye too strong or fat/lye mixed at wrong temperatures
- Cut up, re-melt, re-stir vigorously

White powdery surface:
- Soda ash — harmless, just cosmetic
- Caused by exposure to air during setting
- Wipe or plane off surface before use

Soap burns skin after curing 4 weeks:
- Lye too strong originally
- Cure longer (2-3 more months) — excess lye neutralizes over time
- Or accept as scrubbing soap for objects only, not skin

Grainy or lumpy texture:
- Temperature too cold when mixing
- Not a problem for function — still works as soap

## Accelerated Method for Emergency Soap

When you need soap in 24 hours not 4 weeks:

1. Make lye extra-strong (egg floats 75% exposed)
2. Use very clean rendered fat
3. Cook soap in pot over low heat, stirring constantly 2-3 hours
4. Soap is ready when very thick and pulls from sides of pot
5. This is "hot process" soap — saponification completes during cooking
6. Still caustic immediately after — let cool and use with caution
7. Safer to use for surfaces rather than skin until rested 48 hours

## Soap Without Fat — Saponin Plants

When no fat is available, several plants contain natural saponins that act like soap.

Soapwort (Saponaria officinalis):
- Found in Europe, North America, Asia — common roadside plant
- Broad oval leaves, pink flowers
- Boil leaves and roots in water 20 minutes
- Use cooled liquid as shampoo and gentle soap
- Lower cleaning power than true soap but genuinely effective

Yucca (Southwestern North America):
- Crush roots, add water, whip to create lather
- Native American traditional soap
- Very effective for hair and skin

Horse chestnut (Aesculus hippocastanum):
- Crush seeds, boil in water
- Effective laundry soap
- Do not ingest — seeds are toxic internally

Soapbark tree (Quillaja saponaria):
- South American — bark shavings in water create good lather
- Exported historically as commercial soap ingredient

## Uses Beyond Personal Hygiene

Soap is useful far beyond washing hands:
- Wound cleaning (diluted) — reduces infection dramatically
- Insect repellent — spread thin layer on exposed skin
- Lubricant for saws and tools
- Thread lubricant for hand sewing through leather
- Fire ant and pest control (soapy water suffocates insects)
- Identify gas leaks — soapy water bubbles at leak points
- Surface treatment to slow wood rot
